01 / 04Who we want

We are not looking for people who already know how.

A first-year team cannot recruit on experience, because almost nobody has any. What we can recruit on is follow-through — the willingness to take a small, boring, specific task and actually finish it by the date you said.

If you have built things before, good, you will move fast and end up teaching. If you have never opened a CAD file, also good: the onboarding track assumes exactly that.

A

Engineering & computing

Mechanical, electrical, civil, environmental, software, data. Structures, avionics, propulsion, guidance and payload all start here.

B

Business & communication

Sponsorship, budget, media, outreach and documentation sit in Mission Operations, and the program does not fly without them.

C

Design & the undecided

Brand, identity, photography, video — and anyone who simply wants to find out what they are good at. Rotate and see.

02 / 04Onboarding

Five steps to the range.

Roughly your first eight weeks.

  1. 01

    Show up to an interest meeting

    No application, no interview, no resume. Come to a general body meeting, watch what the divisions are arguing about that week, and decide whether you want in.

  2. 02

    Run the onboarding build

    Every new member builds and flies a small low-power rocket in their first few weeks. It is the fastest way to learn the vocabulary, the safety culture, and whether you like this.

  3. 03

    Rotate two divisions

    Spend a couple of weeks each with two divisions that interest you. You are not committing to anything yet, and you are allowed to discover you were wrong about what you liked.

  4. 04

    Take a task on the vehicle

    Pick a home division and own a real deliverable on the flight article — a part, a board, a procedure, a test. Small scope, real consequences, a lead reviewing your work.

  5. 05

    Fly it

    Be on the range team for launch day. Everyone who put work into the vehicle is there when it goes up, and everyone walks the recovery field.

04 / 04FAQ

Questions we get.

Do I need to be an engineering major?
No. Most of the team is engineering or computing, but the divisions that keep a rocket legal, funded and documented need people from business, communication, marketing, environmental science and design. If you want to be useful here, there is a seat.
I have never built anything. Is that a problem?
It is the normal starting point. This is a first-year club — almost nobody on the roster has flown a high-power rocket before. The onboarding build exists precisely so that your first time touching hardware is not on the flight article.
How much time does it actually take?
Plan on one general body meeting plus one division work session per week, roughly four to six hours. It climbs before a review gate or a launch. Leads carry more. We would rather you commit to a small scope and hit it than over-promise in September and vanish in October.
Is any of this dangerous?
High-power rocketry is regulated, and we operate inside that regulation — certified launch sites, a range safety officer, NFPA 1127 procedures, and supervised handling of energetics. Safety training comes before you touch anything that stores energy. Nobody flies outside a sanctioned range.
Can I get certified to fly my own rockets?
Yes. We run a Tripoli Level 1 and Level 2 certification track and will walk you through the build, the paperwork and the cert flight. Several members will certify this season.
Does this count for credit or look good for internships?
It does not carry course credit. It does give you a documented hardware project, a design review record and a flight you can talk about in detail — which is the part recruiters actually probe in interviews.
I am a graduate student / faculty member. Can I fly a payload?
Yes, that is an explicit goal of the payload division. AQ-1 carries a documented payload bay with a published mechanical and electrical interface. Email us and we will send the interface control document.
