Aquila‑1

Go for build

AQ-1 is intentionally conservative. A commercial reloadable motor, a proven fin geometry, and a fiberglass airframe we can actually finish. The ambition is spent where the learning is: redundant recovery, student-designed avionics, and a real launch-operations cadence with review gates the whole team has to pass.

Subsystems

SS-01

Recovery

Dual deployment. Drogue at apogee, main at 600 ft AGL. Redundant altimeters on separate power and separate charges — either one alone can bring the vehicle home.

Ground tested
SS-02

Avionics

COTS altimeter as flight-critical primary, plus a student-built STM32 logger recording barometric, IMU and GPS data at 100 Hz for post-flight reconstruction.

Bench integration
SS-03

Telemetry

915 MHz LoRa downlink to a laptop ground station with live altitude, battery state and a packet-loss readout. Non-flight-critical by design.

In development
SS-04

Payload bay

A six-inch instrumented section on a documented mechanical and electrical interface, so other FGCU labs can fly something without redesigning the rocket.

02 / 04Flight profile

Nominal profile, liftoff to touchdown.

Simulated in OpenRocket and cross-checked against an independent 6-DOF model. Roughly three minutes of flight for two semesters of work.

  1. T-00:45:00

    Arm avionics

    Continuity check on both altimeters, ejection charges installed at the pad.

  2. T-00:05:00

    Pad clear

    Range declares the pad clear; RSO gives the count.

  3. T+00:00.0

    Ignition

    Motor comes up to pressure; vehicle leaves an 8 ft 1515 rail at roughly 72 ft/s.

  4. T+00:02.9

    Burnout

    Motor cutoff. Coast phase begins at approximately Mach 0.62.

  5. T+00:18

    Apogee / drogue

    Primary altimeter fires the drogue charge within 0.5 s of apogee.

  6. T+01:36

    Main deployment

    Main canopy at 600 ft AGL, targeting a 17 ft/s descent rate.

  7. T+03:05

    Touchdown

    Recovery team walks the predicted drift ellipse with GPS and RDF backup.

04 / 04Research

The thing that comes back down.

Parachutes are solved. Landing under power is not — at least not at our scale, on our budget, with our headcount. That makes it the most interesting problem available to us, and it is the reason this club is called DeepTech and not Rocket Club.

Vertex is the long research thread: a low-altitude hop vehicle that has to sense its own state, steer its own thrust, and put itself down on its legs. We are treating it as a multi-year control-systems problem, and the work starts now in simulation.

R-01

State estimation

Fusing IMU, barometer and GPS into a pose estimate good enough to close a landing loop on, and honest about when it is not.

R-02

Thrust vector control

A two-axis gimbal, servo characterisation, and the actuator lag budget that decides whether the controller is stable at all.

R-03

Guidance

Convex trajectory optimisation for powered descent, run offline first, then trimmed until it fits on the flight computer.

R-04

Landing structure

Deployable legs, touchdown load attenuation, and a test rig that drops the article long before anything flies.
